I have run identity and privileged access programs, built the practice and the commercial model around them, and answered to the auditors who test both. The three sections below run in that order, because that is the order in which the problem gets harder.

Identity and access

Workforce access and privileged access are the same problem seen from two sides: the account a person signs in with, and the account that signs in with nobody watching. I have run both for more than twenty years, from Active Directory in 2003 to Entra ID and Okta today.

On the workforce side that means the program itself: roadmap, operating model and governance for an identity function that has grown by accretion rather than by design. I work vendor-neutral across Microsoft Entra and Okta, both delivered, and Ping Identity in a design capacity.

On the privileged side it means standing privilege reduction with the control evidence to show it held, across CyberArk, BeyondTrust and Delinea, all three implemented rather than evaluated. And it means non-human identity: the service principals, app registrations, managed identities and credentials that carry real permissions and usually carry no owner. At one multi-acquisition insurance client I inventoried 1,697 service principals, reconciled an ownership register to the directory, removed about 200 that were no longer in use, and fully audited another 100 that held advanced permissions.

Underneath sits the plumbing: five Active Directory domains and 10,000-plus users consolidated into one tenant, about 75 service accounts moved from static credentials to Group Managed Service Accounts, and certificate services built from scratch at two employers and operated ever since.

The practice, vendors and capital

I run a professional services practice of ten people across the United States, Argentina and India, grown from four. The operating budget is revenue-linked, and the hiring and termination decisions sit with me. Building a team is harder than inheriting one, and the people I have kept are the ones who could run the work without me.

The practice is over a million dollars a year, roughly half of it recurring by design rather than by luck, because the work is built to renew. The judgement behind that number is what to buy, what to consolidate and what to refuse. I have declined revenue to protect delivery quality, including a service line I was the only person able to deliver, and I would do it again. Anyone can quote a saving. Turning revenue down costs something, which makes it the more honest evidence of how I price risk.

Risk and assurance

Control evidence is where identity stops being an architecture and starts being an accountability. At Liberty Latin America I operated the identity controls tested under SOX at a publicly traded company, and at TaxSlayer I operated the controls tested under PCI DSS in a cardholder environment. Both times I wrote the tests the auditors ran, supplied the evidence, sat with the auditors and remediated the findings. Not as the auditor and not as the compliance owner. My name was never on the signing line.

Above the evidence sits the judgement about what matters. I have sequenced remediation by annual loss expectancy rather than by discovery order, which produces a different list and usually a shorter one, and I have documented the assumptions where client actuals were unavailable. I have owned the identity findings an audit raised against a client, run a monthly governance report carrying a named risk register to client executives, and reported in a four-quadrant format whose security quadrant asks one trend question: is this shrinking or drifting.

And one thing I would offer on its own: due-diligence readiness, scoping identity and access exposure before an acquirer or an auditor does it for you.
